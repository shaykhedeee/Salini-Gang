Research Framework

Research Questions & Motivation

Three sub-questions decompose the primary inquiry; a secondary question on interest-rate transmission was ultimately found statistically insignificant.

01
📊
Sub-Question 1 — Subsidies

Does subsidy growth significantly accelerate India's fiscal deficit growth rate? Food, fuel, and fertiliser subsidies represent the largest discretionary spending category.

02
🏗️
Sub-Question 2 — Capital Expenditure

Is capital expenditure growth fiscally self-financing through GDP multiplier effects? Does productive investment generate sufficient tax revenue to offset its direct cost?

03
🔄
Sub-Question 3 — Debt Cycle

Are interest payments an independent cause or a lagged consequence of India's fiscal deficit? Do they perpetuate a self-reinforcing debt spiral?

⚠️
Note on Secondary Question

Whether fiscal deficit growth independently impacts interest rates was found statistically insignificant (p > 0.05) once the full model is estimated. Analysis was accordingly re-centred on the primary question.

Methodology

Econometric Approach & Specification

Log-ratio transformation ensures stationarity and yields directly interpretable elasticity coefficients.

1

Stationarity: Removes the upward trend in absolute fiscal series, reducing spurious regression risk inherent in non-stationary time series.

2

Scale Normalisation: Makes variables comparable across years regardless of nominal GDP growth — ₹ crore figures are rendered dimensionless.

3

Interpretability: Coefficients represent elasticities: % change in GFD growth per 1% change in driver growth.

4

Two Models: Model 1 regresses GFD on all drivers; Model 2 reverses causality — regressing Interest Payments on GFD to test the debt spiral.

Model 1 — Full Regression Equation
ln(GFDt/GFDt−1) =
  β₀ + β₁·ln(TaxRevt/TaxRevt−1)
  + β₂·ln(Interestt/Interestt−1)
  + β₃·ln(Subsidiest/Subsidiest−1)
  + β₄·ln(CapExt/CapExt−1)
  + β₅·ln(Defencet/Defencet−1) + εt
Model 2 — Reverse Regression (Debt Spiral Test)
ln(Interestt/Interestt−1) =
  β₀ + β₁·ln(GFDt/GFDt−1) + εt

Key Endogeneity Finding
Interest payments are individually significant in Model 1 (β = 0.201, p = 0.047) but lose all significance in the full model (p = 0.143). This reveals that interest payments are endogenous — a consequence of past borrowing, not an independent cause of the deficit.

Key Findings

Summary of Empirical Findings

Four robust conclusions emerge from twenty years of Central Government data.

📈
Finding 1: Subsidies are the Primary Expenditure Driver
Subsidy growth is the single most significant driver of fiscal deficit growth, consistent and robust across all model specifications. Each percentage point acceleration in subsidy spending produces a near-equivalent deterioration in the deficit.
β = +0.550 · p = 0.0002 · sig. at 0.1% level
💰
Finding 2: Tax Revenue is the Primary Stabilising Force
Tax revenue growth is the dominant stabiliser, with a greater-than-proportionate negative effect on deficit growth, reflecting India's improving tax compliance strength and the broadening of the tax base over the study period.
β = −1.647 · p = 0.012 · sig. at 5% level
🏗️
Finding 3: Capital Expenditure is Dynamically Self-Correcting
Capital expenditure growth is associated with fiscal improvement, validating the multiplier hypothesis — productive investment generates GDP and tax revenue growth that offsets its direct cost. Post-COVID capex expansion is not fiscally reckless.
β = −0.436 · p = 0.050 · sig. at 5% level
🔗
Finding 4: Interest Payments are Endogenous — Not Causal
Interest payments are significant in isolation but lose all explanatory power in the full model. They are the transmission mechanism of the debt spiral — a lagged invoice of past borrowing — not its originating cause. Debt management alone cannot fix the deficit.
Model 1: p = 0.143 (n.s.) · Model 2: β = +0.062, p = 0.047

Conclusion

India's fiscal deficit is not a revenue failure — it is an expenditure composition problem.

"Fiscal consolidation in India requires not less spending, but better-composed spending — aggressively slowing unproductive expenditure while sustaining capital investment."

Policy Implications
🎯 Priority 1 — Subsidy Rationalisation
Controlling subsidy growth at its source is the most direct lever. The post-2014 shift to Direct Benefit Transfers (DBT) represents steps in this direction; continuation remains India's most credible path to structural fiscal balance.
📈 Priority 2 — Protect Capital Expenditure
Capital investment should not be sacrificed for consolidation — empirical evidence confirms it is dynamically self-financing. The post-COVID infrastructure push is consistent with sustainable fiscal management.
🏛️ Priority 3 — FRBM & Revenue Broadening
Structural fiscal sustainability requires broadening India's tax base and improving compliance. Revenue-side strengthening is the second most powerful deficit stabiliser (β = −1.647) in the empirical model.
